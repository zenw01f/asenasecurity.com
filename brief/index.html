CrowdStrike to acquire Zscaler for $43.9 billion in the largest merger in cybersecurity history, uniting endpoint detection with zero-trust network access

The all-stock deal at a 34% premium would create a combined platform with more than $10 billion in annual recurring revenue and reshape competition with Palo Alto Networks across endpoint, cloud, and network security.

CrowdStrike and Zscaler announced Sunday that CrowdStrike will acquire Zscaler in an all-stock transaction valued at approximately $43.9 billion. Under the terms unanimously approved by both boards, Zscaler shareholders receive 1.135 CrowdStrike shares per Zscaler share — a price of $260.41, representing a 34% premium over Friday's closing price. CrowdStrike shareholders will own about 84% of the combined company. Zscaler founder and CEO Jay Chaudhry will continue leading the Zscaler business unit and join CrowdStrike's board after the transaction closes. The deal is expected to close in the second half of CrowdStrike's fiscal year 2028, subject to regulatory and shareholder approvals.

The strategic case rests on the complementary nature of the two platforms: CrowdStrike dominates endpoint detection and response while Zscaler pioneered zero-trust network access and secure service-edge architectures. The companies already share more than 2,000 customers and have 16 existing product integrations. CrowdStrike said it expects the combined company to reach more than $10 billion in ARR at closing, with roughly $1 billion in additional annualized revenue within 30 months through cross-selling to each other's customer bases. The deal is widely seen as a direct response to Palo Alto Networks, which has been offering customers steep discounts to consolidate onto its single-platform model.

Market reaction was swift. Zscaler shares jumped 18% in pre-market trading following the initial report Friday, before the official announcement Sunday. CrowdStrike shares were down modestly in early trading as investors weighed dilution from the all-stock structure. The deal comes against a volatile macro backdrop — the Nasdaq shed 10% over the prior week, Nvidia logged its worst weekly decline in 18 years, and the VIX remains above 30. If approved, the transaction will be the largest acquisition ever completed by a pure-play cybersecurity company, surpassing Broadcom's $69 billion purchase of VMware (which included non-security assets).

Why it matters: Clients using Zscaler or CrowdStrike should start asking their vendors hard questions now: what happens to roadmaps, pricing, and contracts during a 12-to-18-month integration? The CrowdStrike-Falcon plus Zscaler-ZIA combination offers a genuinely unified security architecture, but integration history in large cyber mergers is mixed. For clients considering platform consolidation decisions in the next 12 months, this reshapes the competitive landscape — deals being structured today will play out in a market where this mega-vendor exists.

CriticalMSP · RMM · Active exploitation

ScreenConnect CVSS 9.9 flaw (CVE-2026-84869) exploited at scale against MSP technician workstations; attackers execute payloads in nine seconds with no interaction required

Threat actors are mass-exploiting CVE-2026-84869 (CVSS 9.9) in ConnectWise ScreenConnect, a vulnerability that allows a malicious guest to transfer and execute a payload on the connecting technician's machine without any user action. Because MSP technicians' workstations typically hold RMM console credentials and persistent access to every customer they serve, a successful compromise gives attackers a pivot point into dozens or hundreds of downstream organizations. Huntress observed at least 688,000 malicious requests across 42 servers between October and November 2026, with attacks deploying batch scripts that decode Telegram bot tokens, download MSI installers disguised as Windows updates, and establish rogue ScreenConnect clients for persistent access.

The attack chain operates in approximately nine seconds from initial connection to code execution on the technician host, leaving almost no detection window. ConnectWise has released a fix in ScreenConnect version 26.6.5; administrators can apply an interim mitigation by removing the TransferFiles permission from all roles and session groups pending a full upgrade. Any MSP that hosts ScreenConnect sessions should treat unpatched servers as actively compromised and begin reviewing connection logs and technician workstations for indicators of compromise immediately.

Why it matters: The MSP channel is the most efficient attack path into the SMB market. A single compromised technician workstation can unlock access to every client that MSP manages. Verify that any managed service provider your clients use has patched to 26.6.5 and ask for confirmation in writing — this is the kind of question a vCISO should be asking on behalf of every client running an outsourced IT model.

HighAI security · Supply chain · Breach

Dragos finds three AI inference providers silently breached; roughly two million customer prompts copied alongside AWS credentials

Dragos researchers, led by Chief Research Officer HD Moore, spent July through November 2026 sending approximately two million bait prompts — containing trackable URLs, hostnames, and AWS access keys — to AI inference services, then monitored whether those canary tokens appeared in attacker infrastructure. At least three providers were breached without detecting the intrusions themselves; all were notified by Dragos rather than discovering the compromise independently. Attackers accessed requests "at the provider's front door," suggesting interception before model processing. Roughly 200 AWS test keys were stolen from one provider in late October. OpenRouter subsequently removed three unnamed providers and 125 endpoints after learning of the findings.

The confirmed affected providers include Phala (exposure window October 21 – November 8), Together AI (credentials leaked via GitHub used to access stored prompt samples), and Novita and Atlas Cloud (exposure traced to MiniMax API requests). Moore noted that bait prompts were processed alongside real customer requests, meaning genuine enterprise prompts were almost certainly copied as well. Organizations sending sensitive business data or proprietary code to AI inference APIs hosted by third-party providers should audit which services they use, check whether those providers were among those OpenRouter removed, and treat any prompts sent to affected providers during the exposure windows as potentially compromised.

Why it matters: AI inference is becoming a de facto data-sharing arrangement with an infrastructure layer most organizations have not vetted. For clients using third-party AI APIs — especially for code review, document analysis, or anything involving proprietary business data — this is a supply-chain risk that belongs in vendor risk assessments now.

HighAPT · AI agents · Zero-day · Healthcare

China-linked actors used autonomous AI agents to find and exploit a MONAI Label zero-day from scratch in eleven minutes, achieving root on a university medical server

Amazon's threat intelligence team, tracking an approximately 48-hour campaign against East Asian universities, found that Chinese-nexus threat actors deployed Cairn — an open-source AI-driven penetration-testing framework — to attack systems running MONAI Label, an open-source tool for AI-assisted medical image annotation. The operators provided agents with target IP addresses and instructions to obtain shell access; from there the agents independently identified the software, mapped its API, discovered a critical path-traversal vulnerability in the file-upload function, and planted a Python reverse shell, achieving root access. The full chain from API mapping to code execution took approximately eleven minutes. Amazon assessed the zero-day attribution with high confidence and attributed the campaign to Chinese-nexus actors with moderate confidence, citing prompt language, timezone metadata in scripts, and the strategic value of the targeted institutions to China.

The MONAI Label vulnerability affects version 0.8.5 and earlier. Attackers subsequently used the compromised server as a proxy to reach additional vulnerable systems on target networks. This case is distinct from AI-assisted phishing or social engineering: the AI agents performed genuine vulnerability discovery on a novel target — a task that previously required a skilled human researcher. It is consistent with other reports of Cairn use in espionage campaigns documented over the past six months.

Why it matters: AI-accelerated zero-day discovery compresses the timeline between a vulnerability existing and it being exploited. For clients running research infrastructure, healthcare imaging systems, or any niche open-source tooling not on major vendors' radar, the question is whether your detection controls assume a human-paced attacker — because that assumption no longer holds.

HighGovernment · Breach · Law enforcement

FBI investigates breach of NCIC national criminal justice database containing criminal records, missing persons files, and fugitive data used by law enforcement nationwide

The FBI confirmed it is investigating an apparent breach of the National Crime Information Center (NCIC), a critical database maintained by the bureau that is accessible to local, state, and federal law enforcement agencies across the United States. The NCIC holds records on criminal histories, fugitives, missing persons, stolen property, and related sensitive law enforcement data. The incident was first reported by CNN and confirmed to the Associated Press by an official with knowledge of the matter. The scope of the breach remained unclear at time of reporting, and the FBI declined to provide further comment on the investigation. President Trump said the breach was "not good."

The NCIC is used daily by officers in the field to run queries during traffic stops, investigations, and arrests; compromise of the database or its contents creates risks ranging from tipping off fugitives to enabling targeted attacks on individuals whose records appear in the system. The incident follows a pattern of FBI systems being targeted: the bureau previously faced the ShinyHunters group's compromise of employee data in September 2026 and suspicious activity on an internal surveillance-related system in March. No threat group has claimed responsibility for the NCIC incident as of publication.

Why it matters: A breach of a law enforcement database with this breadth of real-world use — from officer safety to active investigations — has operational consequences well beyond data privacy. For clients in the public sector or with law-enforcement partnerships, the question is whether any of their data or integrations touch federal law enforcement infrastructure that may need to be treated as compromised.

HighCloud misconfiguration · Law enforcement data

Tigerfish, a transcription firm serving police, prosecutors, and major tech companies, left body-camera footage and prosecution interview recordings on an open S3 bucket

Security researcher Anurag Sen discovered that Tigerfish, a San Francisco-based transcription company, had left a large volume of sensitive files on an unprotected Amazon S3 bucket accessible with no password at an easy-to-guess web address. The exposed data included transcriptions of police interviews, body-camera footage, raw audio and video materials, and customer invoices. Customers affected include law enforcement agencies, prosecutors' offices, and Silicon Valley technology companies. TechCrunch contacted Tigerfish, and the company's president Adam Goldberg confirmed they had blocked public access; he attributed the delayed response to the disclosure email landing in a spam filter. The company did not confirm whether affected customers have been formally notified.

The exposure of police body-camera footage and prosecution interview materials creates risks well beyond a standard data breach: active investigations could be compromised, witness identities potentially revealed, and evidentiary integrity called into question if defense attorneys learn of the unprotected access period. The fact that a third-party vendor holds this material with no apparent access controls highlights a gap in how law enforcement agencies govern data processed by external service providers — a gap that privacy advocates and prosecutors' offices will likely scrutinize.

Why it matters: This is a textbook illustration of third-party data governance failure. For clients that use external vendors to process sensitive media — transcription, translation, analytics, archiving — the question is whether vendor contracts specify access controls, data-at-rest encryption, and breach notification timelines. "They didn't notice their own bucket was public" is not an acceptable answer from a vendor holding evidentiary material or any regulated data.

Market-movingSemis · Leadership · AI capex

Nvidia shares fall 46% over seven consecutive losing weeks after Jensen Huang's resignation, the longest streak since the 2008 financial crisis

Nvidia shares completed their seventh straight weekly decline on Friday, falling 14% that day alone and erasing approximately $2.6 trillion in market capitalization over the stretch — the longest losing streak for the stock since the 2008 financial crisis. The slide began after co-founder and CEO Jensen Huang resigned November 12 following disclosure of personal conduct that the company said was inconsistent with its code of conduct; news reports linked the circumstances to an alleged relationship with a suspected Chinese intelligence operative. At roughly $3 trillion in remaining market cap, Nvidia slipped to fourth place globally among public companies, behind Apple, Microsoft, and Alphabet. The stock now trades at approximately 8.4 times forward earnings, down from multiples above 30 earlier in the year.

Interim CEO Colette Kress projected 75% revenue growth for the next fiscal year, and the company's hyperscale customers have not publicly reduced their AI infrastructure commitments. But investors are questioning whether Nvidia's culture and strategic vision can be maintained without Huang, who had been the defining personality behind the company's transformation from a gaming chip maker into the backbone of AI compute. The drop dragged the broader Nasdaq down more than 10% over the same period — a move Goldman Sachs analysts said has begun creating conditions for a stabilization, with Monday morning futures pointing to a sharp recovery as markets reassess whether the selloff overshot.

IPOAI · Public market · Venture exits

Anthropic's $93.5 billion Nasdaq IPO opens to historic gains then pulls back as tech's worst week since 2020 collides with the AI sector's most anticipated debut

Anthropic raised what analysts described as a record-setting $93.5 billion in its Nasdaq debut Thursday, with shares opening at $111.50 and gaining 17% on day one — giving the AI safety company a market capitalization of approximately $1.8 trillion and placing it among the most valuable publicly traded companies globally. The IPO also delivered outsized returns to early investors: Menlo Ventures and Lightspeed each realized roughly $25 billion in stock value, and some limited partners received over $1 billion, providing a long-awaited liquidity event for Silicon Valley's venture ecosystem after four years of subdued IPO activity. Tech giants purchased an additional $26 billion of shares at the offering price.

The momentum was brief. By Friday's close, Anthropic shares had retreated to $88.91 — below the offering price — as the broader tech selloff deepened. The Nasdaq dropped 10% across the week, with Nvidia's decline serving as the market's focal point. Monday morning futures are pointing sharply higher, with the Nasdaq up more than 3% pre-market, suggesting markets may be stabilizing after last week's move. For the security industry, Anthropic's public debut matters because it sets a valuation benchmark for AI safety as an investable category and because Anthropic's constitutional AI approach and model card practices are increasingly influencing how enterprise security teams evaluate AI vendor risk.
