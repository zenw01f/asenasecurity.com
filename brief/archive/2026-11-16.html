FBI and DOJ disrupt North Korean FaceStealer campaign behind $400 million in crypto theft; $1.2 billion seized

BlueNoroff actors used deepfake video calls to socially engineer cryptocurrency executives, with each victim's compromised accounts becoming launchpads for the next wave.

U.S. federal authorities seized the cloud infrastructure behind the North Korean BlueNoroff group's FaceStealer campaign, which over roughly 18 months stole more than $400 million in cryptocurrency from 677 targets across 58 countries. The Department of Justice separately announced seizure and restraint of more than $1.2 billion tied to TraderTraitor actors following the related Binance exchange breach. The FBI's takedown exposed more than 900 secretly recorded webcam videos of tech workers on attacker servers.

The attack chain began when operators hijacked a victim's Telegram account, then used it to invite that person's contacts to fake investment video calls. Meeting links — using typosquatted domains such as "uu01webzoom[.]us" — led to sessions where attackers played pre-recorded footage of trusted contacts alongside deepfakes built from YouTube interviews and conference talks. When audio appeared to fail, victims were directed to click links that installed malware harvesting crypto wallets, seed phrases, credentials and social-media access. The compromised contacts then became the starting point for the next wave, creating a self-propagating chain. The FBI noted North Korean actors used what they called "super intelligence" to generate deepfake videos of well-known figures in Web3, making lures harder to dismiss.

Why it matters: Social-engineering defenses built around recognizing faces and voices no longer hold when faces and voices can be fabricated. Advise clients in finance, crypto and high-value executive roles to verify meeting requests through a second channel before joining, never click fix-your-audio links mid-call, and treat any out-of-the-blue investment invitation as suspect regardless of who appears to have sent it.

Fireworks AI breach now linked to both the Uber driver-data theft and last edition's Cursor supply-chain attack

SecurityWeek and TechCrunch report that Uber has confirmed Fireworks AI as the third-party provider behind a breach that exposed names, addresses, driver's license numbers, and government IDs for approximately 150,000 drivers. Fireworks notified Uber on October 9; Uber filed state breach notices on November 13. Fireworks AI is the same provider whose stolen access key led to the trojanized Claude Code extension served through Cursor's marketplace for 11 hours on October 29, covered in the previous edition.

The overlap means a single compromised AI-infrastructure vendor appears to have contributed to two distinct incidents affecting a large tech company and over 100,000 individuals. Uber said the breach did not reach other Uber systems. Neither Fireworks AI nor Uber has described how the credentials were obtained. Fireworks AI is a platform that hosts and fine-tunes open-source AI models.

Why it matters: AI infrastructure vendors now sit in the trust chain for both developer tooling and enterprise data stores. When evaluating vendor risk, ask which AI platforms your suppliers use, what data those platforms can reach, and whether access is scoped to what is strictly needed. Uber's disclosure fits this card rather than the Markets section because the breach is the story, though UBER is a public company.

HighAPT · AI-powered attacks

Chinese APT hacks Barracuda cloud accounts using the Artex AI pentest framework; Google warns fully autonomous attacks are months away

SecurityWeek reports that attackers compromised roughly 10 of Barracuda's cloud accounts between November 1-2, moving from an exposed access key through Kubernetes service accounts to credential exfiltration and a persistent backdoor — the entire campaign took 44 hours. The attackers used the Artex agentic AI framework, primarily driven by Chinese LLMs (DeepSeek v4.1-flash, GLM-5.3, Grok 4.6), which the FBI first warned about in this run's previous edition.

Separately, Google's Threat Intelligence Group published an AI Threat Tracker report on November 16 warning that "unsophisticated attacks requiring little to no human interaction" are likely within three months, and "sophisticated, fully autonomous" agentic attacks within six months. Researchers discovered an exposed staging server running a framework called "exploit_agent" that chains multiple LLMs to find and exploit vulnerabilities automatically. BleepingComputer notes that hundreds of AI-generated proof-of-concept exploits appeared immediately after the PHP2Shell disclosure covered in the previous edition.

Why it matters: The Barracuda incident shows this is already operational, not theoretical. Apply the same hardening that stops human attackers — eliminate hardcoded and default credentials, remove secrets from code artifacts, never expose unauthenticated management interfaces — because automated tools look for exactly these gaps at scale.

HighSupply chain · Hardware

Trezor confirms circuit-board implants in Safe 5 wallets sold by Indonesian reseller CryptoBilis; implants stole recovery phrases via cellular

BleepingComputer and The Hacker News report that Trezor confirmed malicious circuit boards were inserted between the main board and display of Trezor Safe 5 devices purchased from CryptoBilis, a Southeast Asian reseller, between August 20 and September 23, 2026. The implants monitored screen activity during wallet setup or restoration to capture recovery phrases, encrypted the data, and transmitted it over mobile networks. If cellular connectivity was unavailable, the device retained the data and repeatedly retried transmission.

Three devices were confirmed compromised; Trezor found no evidence of tampering in devices from other regional resellers. Firmware 2.13.1 (released November 11) renders these particular implants inactive, but cannot recover phrases already captured. Trezor advises any CryptoBilis customer who has not already migrated to generate a fresh recovery phrase on a new wallet immediately and transfer funds before doing so.

Why it matters: Hardware supply-chain attacks are hard to detect and devastating when they target recovery material. Advise clients holding significant crypto to buy hardware wallets directly from the manufacturer or an authorized retailer, verify firmware at unboxing, and treat any wallet that passed through a third party as potentially compromised.

HighWeb skimmer · PCI

Fandango discloses a four-day payment card skimmer on its checkout page; breach ran October 8–11

BleepingComputer reports that Fandango notified customers of a web-skimmer attack that exposed payment card numbers, CVV codes, expiry dates, billing ZIP codes, and email addresses for anyone who purchased tickets through the site between October 8 and 11, 2026. The company discovered the malicious code on October 30. Names, government IDs, and Social Security Numbers were not in scope. Fandango is offering two years of complimentary credit monitoring, with an enrollment deadline of February 13, 2027.

No threat actor has claimed responsibility and Fandango has not disclosed how the skimmer code was injected. The 19-day gap between the exposure window ending and discovery is typical for Magecart-style attacks, which often go undetected until a card issuer notices patterns. Card data from a 72-hour window can still drive substantial fraud if CVV codes are intact.

Why it matters: Skimmer attacks on e-commerce checkout pages remain one of the most reliable card-theft methods because they intercept data before it is encrypted. PCI DSS 4.0.1 requires script integrity monitoring and behavioral controls for payment-page JavaScript — if any client operates a checkout, verify those controls are in place before year-end.

MediumConfidential computing · AMD

Mior and Skyfall attacks break AMD SEV-SNP VM isolation; full platform patches not until December–April

The Hacker News reports two new academic attacks against AMD's SEV-SNP confidential computing on EPYC server processors. Mior (CVE-2026-0462, CVSS 5.9) exploits the Platform Security Processor startup sequence to leave the Reverse Map Table uninitialized, allowing a malicious hypervisor to read and write a confidential VM's memory and forge attestation reports. Skyfall (CVE-2026-40671, CVSS 4.9) uses instruction-timing side channels to recover disk-encryption keys through cached VM state left over from failed starts.

Both affect EPYC 7003, 8004, 8005, 9004, and 9005 families. AMD released SEV firmware and microcode updates in August; platform firmware fixes are due in December (Skyfall) and by January 2027 (Mior), with embedded-chip patches extending to April 2027. Both attacks require the attacker to control the hypervisor and BIOS, which describes a compromised cloud host rather than a typical enterprise threat. Cloud providers including AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud received private notification in August.

Why it matters: Confidential computing is increasingly used to protect sensitive workloads in shared cloud environments. If clients rely on AMD SEV-SNP as a security boundary, verify their cloud provider has applied the August firmware and microcode, and check timelines for the platform-level patches. The threat model requires a compromised hypervisor — assess whether that scenario is in scope for sensitive workloads.

Post-incidentCloud reliability · Microsoft

Microsoft traces the November 12–13 365 outage to a July bug that lay dormant for four months before cascading through 80% of gateway servers

BleepingComputer and TechCrunch report that Microsoft's post-incident review found a networking gateway defect introduced in a July update that "overwhelmed servers under certain traffic conditions." The bug went undetected because test-lab hardware had fewer CPU cores than production servers; the problem worsens with more cores and newer hardware. It activated November 12 when Teams automatically rerouted traffic to Exchange Online, increasing load on a system already running at 86% capacity due to an unrelated partial outage — causing more than 80% of gateway servers to become unresponsive within under two hours. Attempts to redirect traffic to other regions cascaded the failure. Services took nearly 24 hours to fully restore.

Microsoft subsequently added a phishing-awareness note: attackers quickly launched campaigns using fake Microsoft 365 service-status notices that referenced the real outage to harvest credentials. The company has committed to increased capacity, enhanced monitoring, and new safeguards against cascading redirects.

Why it matters: This outage illustrates two risks at once — a hidden defect that testing missed because lab hardware differed from production, and attackers exploiting the disruption for phishing. Clients who lack out-of-band communication plans for email outages are doubly exposed: unable to reach each other and facing credential-phishing emails that look entirely plausible.
